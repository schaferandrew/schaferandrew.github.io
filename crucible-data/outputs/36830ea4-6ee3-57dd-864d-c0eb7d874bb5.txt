## Top 5 Choices (with detailed rationale)

### 1. Mara Kowalski
Mara works beautifully because it sits at that precise intersection: recognizable as Polish heritage without feeling like a costume piece. The original spelling "Marzena" carries too much baggage for a working-class family in the Upper Midwest trying to navigate assimilation, but "Mara" is short, memorable, and has literary weight (think of its use in Greek mythology). Kowalski is one of Poland's most common surnames, grounding her firmly in working-class authenticity. She's not hiding behind a made-up or exoticized last name; this is someone whose family actually came from the Midwest industrial belt. The dry humor comes through—she'd probably make light of how "Mara" sounds both familiar and slightly off.

### 2. Tatiana Vencovsky
This name signals intelligence and ambition before we even meet her character. Tatiana has a certain European sophistication that suggests she's comfortable with culture, perhaps reading widely or pursuing advanced degrees. The surname Vencovsky is distinctly Polish-Slovakian border region—it's the kind of name that comes from someone whose grandparents worked in coal mining towns. There's a slight exotic edge to it (V- sound, -sky ending) but it's not pretentious; it's honest. She could be from Detroit or Chicago or Milwaukee with this name. The emotional guardedness is interesting here: she chose something slightly uncommon, which suggests someone who thinks carefully about how they present themselves.

### 3. Eliza Wojciechowski
Eliza is such a strong choice because of what it reveals about her family's assimilation arc. Born in the early 1990s to first-generation immigrants or their children, Eliza represents that generation growing up speaking English at home but preserving cultural markers like surnames. It's American enough to feel natural in academic or professional spaces but distinctly Polish when you know what to look for. The last name Wojciechowski is unmistakably Polish—your character might be the kind of person who enjoys explaining Polish words to friends as conversation pieces, dryly correcting misconceptions about their heritage. This feels like someone whose ambition involves moving up through systems that often exoticize or ignore their background.

### 4. Hana Kowalczyk
Short, sharp, memorable—this name suits an emotionally guarded character perfectly. "Hana" (sometimes used as a diminutive for Haná, which itself derives from the Polish male name) was uncommon but not unheard of in assimilated families who wanted something simpler than traditional long-form names. Kowalczyk is a working-class surname common throughout industrial areas; it doesn't sound like something you'd find on an executive floor in Manhattan or Wall Street. This combination tells us she's proud enough to keep her Polish identity visible but grounded enough to not wear it as a costume. The name has a certain literary precision to it—few letters, easy to remember, impossible to mispronounce if someone bothers to try.

### 5. Anna Staszewski
Anna is the most authentic choice because it's exactly what a Polish-American working-class family would have given their daughter in the early 1990s. It was popular then, but not ubiquitous—there was enough distinction that she wouldn't be lost among the crowd. Staszewski is a distinctly Polish surname (of Latin origin, meaning "son of Stanislaus"), which grounds her heritage without being exoticized or caricatured. This name signals someone who grew up in places like Gary, Indiana or Milwaukee with a strong sense of community and perhaps some resentment about how people treat their accent or background. The dry humor would come from watching others misunderstand the name or assume she's somehow "European" rather than American. The ambition is there—it's not an exotic or made-up name designed to stand out; it's someone who earned respect through work and intelligence, not by performing her heritage for strangers.
