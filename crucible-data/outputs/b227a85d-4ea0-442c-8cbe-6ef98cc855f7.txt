A few notes on strategy before the list: a 34-year-old in a contemporary novel was born around 1990, so her first name should come from that era's pool — or be a timeless revival name honoring a grandmother, which is very common in Polish-American families. By the third generation, heritage usually lives in the surname, not the first name, so I've kept first names assimilated and used real, common upper-Midwest Polish surnames (Milwaukee, Chicago, Detroit, Toledo territory) while skipping the punchline names like Kowalski. Sound matters too: clipped, single-syllable first names read as guarded and dry; a few warmer ones give you range.

NAMES:
1. Mara Zielinski — Clipped, slightly armored first name; Zielinski is everywhere in Milwaukee and Chicago without ever reading as a joke.
2. Nicole Sobczak — Exactly what a working-class Midwest family named a daughter in 1990; the polished first name against the blunt surname mirrors her code-switching ambition.
3. Kate Wisniewski — Dry and cool; Wisniewski is unmistakably Polish yet completely ordinary up north, where everyone knows how to say it.
4. June Krol — Spare, literary, self-contained; Krol ("king") is short and quietly proud.
5. Vera Duda — A grandmother-name revival that reads old-soul rather than ethnic; Duda is plain and Midwestern to the bone.
6. Claire Mazur — Claire carries polish and ambition; Mazur (also a Polish dance) keeps the heritage in the room without waving it.
7. Ruth Baran — Severe and intelligent; Baran is unflashy and common.
8. Erica Tokarz — Period-accurate for 1990 and practical rather than pretty; Tokarz is an occupational surname with no romance to it.
9. Dana Sikora — The androgynous first name suits someone who keeps people at arm's length; Sikora is standard-issue in Polish Chicago.
10. Nora Grabowski — Nora is warm, but the surname anchors her hard to place and class.
11. Greta Pawlak — Greta has brains and frost in it; Pawlak is endlessly common in Wisconsin.
12. Lena Wozniak — Lena reads like the Americanized slice of Magdalena — heritage as a whisper, not an announcement.
13. Tess Ostrowski — Unsentimental one-syllable first name; Ostrowski is sturdy and familiar.
14. Irene Czajkowski — Named for a grandmother; a childhood spent spelling Czajkowski for strangers is the kind of thing that builds armor.
15. Anna Tomczak — Plain and self-contained, like the character; Tomczak is solidly Midwestern Polish.
16. Marta Kubiak — Marta works in both languages, quietly ethnic without a stereotype in sight; Kubiak is very Wisconsin.
17. Rachel Kaminski — Era-perfect every-girl name that lets her intelligence be the surprise; Kaminski is common and clean.
18. Helena Sadowski — Helena is the Polish spelling — a quiet inheritance most readers will never clock.
19. Maggie Novak — The anglicized Novak spelling fits someone who has let her heritage go quiet; Maggie is friendly on the surface only.
20. Danielle Jankowski — Utterly unremarkable by design, which is exactly how she likes it; Jankowski is all over the upper Midwest.

TOP FIVE:
1. Mara Zielinski
2. Kate Wisniewski
3. June Krol
4. Nicole Sobczak
5. Vera Duda
