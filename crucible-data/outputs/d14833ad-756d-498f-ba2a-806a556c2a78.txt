A few notes on how I approached this before the lists:

The character is 34 now (born ~1990), so her first name should sit comfortably in the early-'90s American naming pool rather than read as a grandmother's name or a current baby-name-of-the-year fad. For Polish-American working-class families in the upper Midwest, the realistic spectrum runs from fully Anglicized names (families that assimilated) to traditionally Polish ones rendered in English—rarely to ornate, folkloric spellings, which is where "caricature" creeps in. So I leaned on anglicized forms of genuinely common Polish names (Katarzyna→Catherine, Krystyna→Christina, Danuta→Dana) and everyday surnames that are among Poland's most frequent (Nowak, Nowakowski, Kamiński, Szymanski) rather than exotic or aristocratic-sounding ones. I also varied the "level" of Polishness so you can choose how heritage-forward the character should read, and tried to pick names whose *sound* does some emotional work—crisp, plain, or slightly reserved—to match a dry, guarded, ambitious protagonist.

NAMES:
1. Catherine Nowak — Katarzyna anglicized; crisp and no-frills, reads as serious and capable, so she clearly leads with her mind.
2. Christina Szymanski — Krystyna in English dress; the soft "-ina" keeps a faint European resonance while sounding like an ordinary '90s kid.
3. Maggie Kamiński — Magdalena anglicized; an easygoing surface that conceals a guarded interior, ideal dry-wit setup.
4. Dana Mazur — Danuta shortened to its everyday form; short surname and blunt first name signal working-class directness.
5. Joanna Kowalczyk — genuinely both Polish and English at once; "Kowalczyk" (smith's son) is authentic heritage, not costume jewelry.
6. Irene Nowakowski — Irena anglicized; Irene carries an old-world steadiness that suits emotional reserve.
7. Alexandra Dąbrowski — Aleksandra; the grandeur in the name matches ambition and a certain self-possession.
8. Andrea Glowacki — Andrea bridges Polish and English; slightly uncommon in the Midwest, marking her as the observant outsider.
9. Nicole Wozniak — could be Nikola or purely American Nicole; "Wozniak" is quintessential working-class Polish surname.
10. Rachel Nowak — a cross-cultural classic; understated and smart without ever trying hard.
11. Stephanie Wiśniewski — peak-'90s first name grounded by one of Poland's most common surnames.
12. Samantha Lewandowski — popular era name backed by a full-family Polish surname.
13. Krista Jankowski — the sharp "-sta" edge (from Krystina) suits dry humor and guardedness.
14. Lauren Zieliński — Anglicized first name for an assimilated family; "Zielinski" is everyday Polish.
15. Anna Król — Anna works in both languages; Król ("king") gives the plain name quiet weight.
16. Elizabeth Majewski — Elżbieta anglicized; formal and self-serious, fitting a driven woman.
17. Sofia Nowakowska — Zofia modernized to Sofia; bridges generations with a contemporary feel.
18. Katharine Gorski — full form of Kasia; the slightly archaic "Katharine" lends a dry, literary edge.
19. Veronica Nowak — Weronika's English twin; elegant and a little reserved.
20. Barbara Szymkowski — shared Polish/English name; sturdy, unflashy, reliable-sounding.
