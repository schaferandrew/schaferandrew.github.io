## Strongest Five

**Helen** — The American form of Helena, one of the most common Polish women's names. It passes as fully American yet carries real heritage weight. It's quiet, dignified, slightly old-fashioned without being dated — a name that announces nothing, which suits an emotionally guarded woman.

**Greta** — The natural nickname for Margaret/Margit (from Gretka). Two syllables, a hard clipped consonant; it sounds dry and sharp by default. Uncommon enough to be memorable, common enough to be real in a family where grandmothers named people. It threads heritage into a name that never feels costumed.

**Irena** — Genuinely Polish and genuinely Polish-American; it's a real name, not a caricature. In the US it's rare, so it carries quiet gravity. It reads as intelligent, composed, and steady — and it makes the heritage *hers* rather than a prop.

**Kasia (Katarzyna)** — The most authentic choice: Katarzyna is the single most common name in Poland (the equivalent of Katherine), so in a Polish-American family it's completely ordinary. "Kasia" is what her mother would have called her at a 34-year-old's kitchen table. The only risk is being too on-the-nose, but handled without wink it's the truest name on the list.

**Terry** — The Americanized edge of Teresa, a deeply common Polish Catholic name. Terry is slightly rough, working-class, upper-Midwest by instinct — a name that sounds like it could wrench a carburetor and land a dry one-liner in the same afternoon. It gives the character a quietly defiant, gender-neutral steel without a single reference to her background.

**Honorable mentions:** Rose (old-fashioned working-class warmth with a guarded surface) and Ewa (short, soft, distinctly Polish, and very natural for this generation).
